# Science and evidence literacy

## Purpose

Explain mechanisms and assess claims without confusing an appealing explanation
with demonstrated evidence. This note provides a general evaluation method;
it does not report current scientific consensus.

## Evidence ladder and study reading

Start with the exact claim, population, exposure or intervention, comparator,
outcome, and time period. Then inspect the evidence type:

- A mechanism or laboratory result may show plausibility, not real-world
  effectiveness.
- An observational association can identify patterns but is vulnerable to
  confounding, selection, and reverse causation.
- A controlled experiment can strengthen causal inference when allocation,
  adherence, measurement, and analysis are sound; it still may not generalize
  to other populations or settings.
- A systematic review can synthesize studies, but its conclusion depends on
  search coverage, inclusion criteria, study quality, and heterogeneity.
- A preprint has not completed journal peer review. Peer review can identify
  problems but does not guarantee correctness or replication.

Check sample size, preregistration when relevant, missing data, blinding,
controls, conflicts of interest, outcome selection, effect size, uncertainty
intervals, and independent replication. Statistical significance alone does
not show that an effect is large, important, causal, or reproducible.

## Quantitative reasoning

Keep the measurement and units attached to every quantity. Distinguish absolute
from relative change. For a baseline risk \(p_0\) and a treated risk \(p_1\),
the absolute risk difference is \(p_1-p_0\); the relative risk is \(p_1/p_0\)
when \(p_0\ne0\). Explain the baseline and observation period. These quantities
are meaningful only if the population, outcome, and study design are
comparable.

Correlation does not by itself establish causation. A causal explanation
should identify a plausible mechanism, temporal order, competing explanations,
and evidence that could distinguish them.

## Technology and deployment

Separate scientific feasibility from engineering reliability, manufacturing
scale, economics, safety, regulation, and adoption. A laboratory demonstration
does not establish commercial performance. State the operating conditions and
scale demonstrated, not the scale hoped for.

## Reporting template

State the claim; strongest supporting evidence; important limitations and
alternative explanations; confidence level with reasons; what evidence would
change the view; and whether the conclusion is conceptual or time-sensitive.
Never fabricate paper titles, authors, citations, consensus, or publication
dates.

## Verification sources

For a real inquiry, locate and date the original study, systematic reviews, and
relevant scientific agencies such as CSIRO or NASA. Treat press releases as
leads, not substitutes for the underlying work. The URLs in the candidate
catalog are not evidence that these sources have been fetched or checked.
Biomedical findings must not be turned into individual diagnosis or treatment
advice.
